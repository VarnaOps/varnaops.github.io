Values

What we optimize for, and why: reproducibility, adherence to scientific consensus, a balance of accuracy and scalability, unbiased benchmarking, a clear-eyed stance on generative AI, an outcome-based platform, a lean, profitable way of running the company, and where we're headed next.

1. Reproducibility

Reproducibility means an independent party, given the same data and the same documented procedure, arrives at the same result [1]. It is the property that turns a result into evidence. At VarnaOps we are transparent about which workflows we use, how they are configured, and why they were chosen over the alternatives — that transparency is both a scientific obligation and how we build trust with the research community.

Generative AI makes this harder, not easier. An LLM or an agent given the same objective, or even the same prompt, can take any of an unbounded number of paths to get there, and slightly different paths tend to produce different answers. That variability is fine when it's the point — exploring alternatives, stress-testing a hypothesis — but it is corrosive when it enters a production pipeline unnoticed. We use generative AI deliberately, never as an unversioned step in a result researchers rely on.

2. Follow scientific consensus

If many paths can reach a similar result, the practical fix isn't to find the one true path — it's to shrink the space of paths worth taking. Scientific consensus, i.e. a field's accepted best practices, does exactly that: it's the accumulated, peer-reviewed answer to "which of the many reasonable choices actually works." We stay as close as possible to that consensus in our workflows, so results are reproducible by construction and comparable across experiments, labs, and time, rather than tied to one tool's private conventions.

3. Balance accuracy and scalability

Within that consensus we optimize for two things at once: accuracy — results that are correct both biologically and computationally — and scalability, so the same workflow holds up from a pilot dataset to an atlas. Cell annotation, one of our core products, is a case in point: assigning a cell type or state to a cell from its transcriptional profile is, formally, a classification problem, and the field has proposed many ways to solve it. VAE-based methods (scVI/scANVI) remain the state of the art for batch correction and cell-type classification in single-cell data [2,3], and are far cheaper to run than foundation-model or LLM-based approaches, which have repeatedly underperformed on single-cell tasks relative to the compute they demand [4,5,6].

That said, our philosophy is method-agnostic: we use whatever performs best for a given task within our infrastructure, not whatever is fashionable. We don't just wrap pre-trained models unmodified — depending on the task, we train small, efficient models from scratch, or fine-tune larger pre-trained models, tuning either path where we find gaps rather than accepting a vendor's defaults. This also means leaning on open models rather than betting the platform on one proprietary architecture: designing a new model from scratch is a high-variance bet few labs can consistently win, so instead of picking one horse we build on the field's accumulated, peer-reviewed progress — open source is still the dominant source of innovation in this field, for the same reproducibility reasons described above.

4. Trust independent benchmarks, verify internally

Comparing model performance is genuinely hard, and a new method's reported numbers are often — consciously or not — optimistic. We start from unbiased, independent benchmarks to shortlist candidates for a given task, then run our own internal benchmarks for two purposes: choosing which model to deploy, and tuning it for performance. Our current scVI/scANVI configuration, for example, departs from the textbook training parameters and loss function in specific, measured ways; that final configuration isn't proprietary folklore — it's documented in our reference reports and reproducibility pages. Other configurations are tried and set aside internally along the way, for under-performing or not scaling, before a model ever reaches production. Nothing here is final: workflows evolve as the field advances and as user feedback points us toward real improvements — that input is essential, not an afterthought — and we track that evolution internally with versioning, changelogs, and a containerized build, so a result stays reproducible on our platform even after the stack underneath it has changed.

5. Our stance on generative AI

The platform is not a computational engine conjured fresh by a prompt. It pre-exists, has been extensively validated, and changes only when the field advances or a bug is found — that's what keeps it reproducible, close to best practices, accurate, and scalable. Generative AI has a real role at VarnaOps, just not as the analysis engine itself: we use LLMs and agentic tooling for brainstorming, for writing and reviewing our own code, for speeding up day-to-day development and business operations, for parsing and formatting inputs, and for generating custom interpretations of results that are already computed by the validated pipeline.

6. Code-free, outcome-based

Anyone who works with biological data has watched people lose time to fragile pipelines, sprawling notebooks, and open questions about which parameter or workflow to use — all of it eating into time-to-insight and, often, into reproducibility. Our goal is a platform where researchers start from the conclusion they want to draw, not from the infrastructure required to get there: drag in data, get results, end to end. Removing the pipeline from the researcher's task also makes results easier to compare across datasets and labs, because everyone ran the same validated path.

7. A lean, profitable business

Most drug-discovery and bio-AI companies aren't profitable for years — that's not a flaw, it reflects the genuine, high-risk, upfront cost of designing, testing, and approving a drug. VarnaOps isn't a drug-discovery company. We're SaaS and infrastructure for biology: low risk, with profitability built into how we operate rather than deferred to some future scale. We don't own the data we run on (most of it is public, under licenses that permit our use) or the biological conclusions drawn from it — we own the orchestration and the tuning. We stay lean, fully remote, and scale-to-zero on cloud infrastructure, which keeps fixed costs low and prices competitive, and lets us scale up on demand once volume justifies it. We're in the trust business, and reproducibility and transparency are our wedge. Our products are built for the broad research community first, and we're open to partnering with individual labs, biotech, pharma, agtech, and environmental-science groups on customized work.

8. Where we're headed

Today we're focused entirely on the single-cell biology vertical, and our priority is finishing what we've started — fully productionizing an end-to-end pipeline, from reference construction through annotation, benchmarking, and reporting. Only once that's done will we look at expanding, one vertical at a time. Spatial biology, twice named Nature Methods' Method of the Year [7,8], is the natural next vertical we will focus on: it's the same underlying downstream-analysis problem, just applied to data that keeps each cell's position in the tissue. Other verticals will follow the same pattern in time.
